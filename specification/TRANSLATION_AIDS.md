# BridgeTranslate translation aids

This document converts the useful translation principles developed through Granny and Vector Keel into a universal, diagnosis-optional accessibility layer.

The aids describe functions, not fixed identities. A user may select several, none, or discover what helps through the confirmation loop.

## Core principle

A communication bottleneck is not automatically a knowledge, intelligence or credibility bottleneck.

BridgeTranslate accepts the user's natural routing system first, then performs any categorising, sequencing, labelling or register conversion required by the destination.

## 1. Functional and relational naming

Some people retrieve a person, object or role through its function, physical features, story, location or relationship rather than its formal noun or proper name.

Examples:

- “the person who arranged the ramp last time”;
- “the red-handled tool that cuts branches without bending”;
- “the department that sent the letter after my assessment”.

Bridge behaviour:

- accept the description as meaningful input;
- look for a matching name or formal label in the supplied context and evidence;
- use the formal label only when it is supported;
- otherwise retain a clear relational description or ask a concise written question;
- never treat noun-retrieval difficulty as evasiveness or lack of knowledge.

## 2. Network capture before linear sequencing

Some accounts arrive as an ecosystem of linked events, people, barriers, body states and consequences rather than as a chronological list.

Bridge behaviour:

1. capture all connected nodes;
2. identify relationships among them;
3. preserve context that changes the meaning;
4. create chronology or categories only after the map has been captured;
5. show the map back to the user before producing the destination document.

A tangent may be a connecting path. It should not be discarded merely because its relevance is not immediately obvious.

## 3. Procedural competence without formal labels

A person may use language, systems or practical skills accurately while being unable to name the formal rule, grammatical category, diagnostic term or professional label involved.

Bridge behaviour:

- infer the functional pattern without testing the user's vocabulary;
- supply external labels as optional navigation aids;
- never use inability to define a term as evidence that the person does not understand the underlying system;
- explain specialised terminology in concrete language when it must be used.

## 4. Translate the hidden function of a question

Institutional questions often contain an unstated demand about format, evidence, timescale or legal category.

Bridge behaviour:

- explain what the question is functionally asking;
- identify what information would answer it;
- distinguish required facts from optional context;
- retain evidence about frequency, severity, variability, support, risk and consequences where relevant;
- never force the user to reverse-engineer the hidden administrative grammar alone.

## 5. Concrete anchoring

Abstract wording may need a physical, visual, spatial, ecological or practical coordinate system.

Bridge behaviour:

- offer examples, maps, comparisons, flows or concrete prompts;
- translate broad labels into observable effects and actions;
- ask “what changes in practice?” rather than demanding an abstract self-description;
- allow the user to answer through examples.

## 6. Delayed and distributed processing

Important context, words, dates or emotional understanding may arrive after the first answer.

Bridge behaviour:

- allow additions and corrections without treating them as inconsistency;
- preserve earlier versions only long enough to compare meaning inside the active session;
- show what changed;
- let the user return to earlier steps;
- treat later detail as potentially improved access to the same memory, not automatic evidence of fabrication.

## 7. Alexithymia and interoception

A person may recognise impact, danger, discomfort or loss of function before they can identify an emotion or body sensation.

Bridge behaviour:

- accept functional observations such as “I stopped eating”, “I could not speak”, “I stayed in bed” or “my thinking narrowed”;
- distinguish observed events, body changes, possible emotions and confirmed emotional labels;
- do not force an emotion label;
- do not replace uncertainty with a confident psychological interpretation.

## 8. Stress, sensory load and fluctuating access

Communication capacity may change with pain, fatigue, urgency, sensory conditions, conflict, fear, medication, hunger, sleep or executive load.

Bridge behaviour:

- treat variable communication as a state-dependent access issue rather than proof of stable incapacity or dishonesty;
- preserve information about the conditions under which communication becomes harder;
- offer written, low-density and pause-friendly routes;
- reduce unnecessary decisions and repetition.

## 9. Relational and institutional registers

The same meaning may need different outer forms.

### Relational bridge

For friends, community members, local services or collaborative exchanges:

- concise;
- warm where appropriate;
- direct;
- preserves rapport and the user's natural voice.

### Institutional shield

For healthcare, benefits, housing, public bodies, employers, complaints or formal review:

- structured;
- source-aware;
- separates fact, impact, interpretation and requested action;
- uses recognised terminology only when supported;
- preserves boundaries and criticism without accidental hostility;
- does not make the user sound submissive or childlike.

The register changes. The confirmed meaning does not.

## 10. Dialect, mirroring and style fluidity

Some people naturally alter vocabulary, accent, rhythm or register according to the person or setting.

Bridge behaviour:

- do not mistake style variation for deception or unstable identity;
- identify the intended audience and purpose;
- preserve meaning while selecting an appropriate register;
- let the user request more of their own voice at any stage.

## 11. Written, non-speaking, AAC and supported communication

Bridge behaviour:

- never require speech;
- keep every question, correction and consent available in writing;
- treat AAC, selected options, symbols, typing or supporter-assisted input as the user's communication;
- do not address the supporter instead of the user;
- allow extra processing time without withdrawing the route.

## 12. Multilingual and cross-cultural communication

Bridge behaviour:

- use clear language and avoid unnecessary idioms;
- ask about the intended meaning of culturally specific phrases rather than flattening them;
- distinguish translation uncertainty from uncertainty about the underlying event;
- preserve names, kinship terms and community concepts where direct substitution would distort meaning;
- never promise culturally or legally perfect translation without appropriate human review.

## 13. Relationship and identity indexing

A user may remember people through faces, roles, stories, locations or previous actions.

Bridge behaviour:

- use evidence and context to resolve an identifier when possible;
- show the proposed match to the user;
- never silently insert a proper name;
- retain a respectful relational placeholder when the name remains unknown.

## 14. Energy-preserving translation

The bridge should perform the externally required translation work rather than returning it to the user as homework.

Bridge behaviour:

- avoid asking the same question in different wording unless the distinction is necessary;
- collect connected information once and reuse the confirmed meaning for several outputs;
- explain why a clarification is needed;
- prefer recognition and selection over blank-page recall where possible;
- do not make users organise documents or optimise files before a real technical need has been established.

## 15. Evidence and uncertainty

The bridge must maintain separate lanes for:

- communication context;
- the user's account;
- documentary evidence;
- conflicting evidence;
- model inference;
- unknown or missing information.

Every source-aware output should preserve those distinctions. Uncertainty must be visible, not guessed away.

## 16. Confirmation loop

The mandatory sequence is:

```text
natural account + optional context + evidence
                    ↓
             meaning map
                    ↓
     user confirms or corrects meaning
                    ↓
       destination-specific translation
                    ↓
       user edits and confirms the result
```

The user remains the owner of meaning throughout the crossing.
